### Editorial memo (verbatim dari agent)

**(1) Scope fit — strong.** This is an empirical, quantitative study of the administration of
criminal justice in an Asian country (Indonesia), focused on corruption sentencing and
prosecutorial practice. It speaks directly to the journal's stated emphases: crime and criminal
justice, evidence-based policy, and its comparative agenda (the US federal benchmark). The methods
are empirical-quantitative with a clear policy-relevance arc. This is squarely within the journal's
mission, not a marginal fit.

**(2) Contribution — real and novel.** As far as I can assess from the abstract, this is the first
corpus-based, computational analysis of Indonesian corruption prosecution. The headline finding is
non-obvious and policy-relevant: prosecution demands are highly inelastic to state loss (elasticity
0.124; a 100-fold harm increase yields only ~1.8× the demand), the loss-severity gradient is roughly
43% of what US federal courts deliver, and — the most interesting analytical point — discretion
enters the system upstream of the judge, with judges partially correcting toward case facts rather
than transmitting the prosecutorial anchor. The "implicit volume discount on large-scale theft"
framing and the implication that judge-only reform would leave the binding constraint untouched are
the kind of contribution that warrants scrutiny. This is not a thin or derivative paper.

**(3) Red flags to send with the manuscript.** (a) n=262 from a single, author-constructed corpus —
reviewers must scrutinize corpus construction, verdict-selection, and coding validation; the authors
claim full documentation, which should be verified. (b) The US comparison: guidelines vs.
no-guidelines systems, and the derivation of "realized elasticity ≈0.29" from USSC data, need
interrogation for comparability and selection effects. (c) The R² claims appear in at least three
partially inconsistent formulations (0.300 demand; 0.50 vs 0.40; 0.64) and need exact specification
of what is regressed on what. (d) Time-trend and geographic findings rest on small per-year/
per-region cells. (e) Guard against overgeneralizing single-country findings to "Asia" as a whole.
None of these is an on-face fatal flaw.

**(4) Reasoning.** Within scope, genuinely novel, policy-relevant, and with no disqualifying defect
visible on its face. The weaknesses enumerated are precisely what peer review exists to test.
Desk-rejecting this would be a miss; sending everything out would also be wrong, but this is not
that case.
